UTMB was the first race in a long time where I showed up with real doubt.

I knew I was not in the best shape of my life. Travel stress, sickness, shin issues, and a bunch of nutrition and hydration mistakes had already made the buildup far from ideal. Even so, I still thought I could put together a solid race.

Instead, I got punched in the mouth almost immediately.

## Training

This was the first time I trained for a planned race while traveling for an extended period.

The original idea was to spend two months in Chamonix training specifically for UTMB. That changed when a friend had a wedding in Madrid in mid-July and I decided I wanted more time in Spain, especially because I was trying to improve my Spanish. So instead of parking myself in one place, I trained while moving around Spain, with some earlier time in South Africa and London mixed in too.

Training was rough from the start.

I was sidelined from late February to mid-May with shin splints. I was still biking and swimming, but I was not really running. After a clean MRI in February and a clean X-ray in May, I basically decided to run through it and see what happened. Some stretching and strength work helped for a while, but the pain kept coming back. It flared up in South Africa in early June, again in Spain in early July, and then once more during the last month before the race badly enough that I nearly convinced myself I had a stress fracture.

I was able to ramp up in June, but I did not get real mountain training until early July when I reached Spain. In South Africa and London, my travel setup did not really allow for hills. Even in Madrid, I sometimes ended up doing repeats on a tiny 70-100 foot hill in a park because that was the only practical option unless I wanted to spend over an hour on a bus.

By mid-to-late July I finally got closer to 10,000 feet of vert in a week, with steep long runs near Madrid and in the Sierra Nevada. Right after one of those long runs, I had a weird spike in resting heart rate for a few days, plus joint pain in my knees, shin, and wrists and a general washed-out feeling. A friend nearby had COVID, and I am pretty sure I got it around six weeks before the race.

I backed off for about a week and then tried to build again, but the timing was bad. I was in southern Spain during a heat wave, dealing with 100+ degree heat, which was not exactly ideal for quality running.

The low point came on a planned 24-mile long run at Montserrat outside Barcelona. I felt weak and achy from the start. I tried to fix it with food and drink from the visitor center, but that did nothing. I ended up abandoning the run at mile 4 and walking down the mountain at a 30-minute pace, pretty convinced UTMB might be slipping away. I went Strava-dark for a while after that just for my own sanity.

Eventually things improved. I got in some solid runs in the Pyrenees and the Basque Country and started to feel more normal. Then I spent three weeks living next to a trail that climbed 2,500 feet in 5K, so 10 miles with 3,000 feet of vert became a pretty routine day.

I did a 50K with 9,000 feet of vert and felt great until around mile 22, when nutrition problems started wrecking me. That had actually been a theme all summer. I kept getting dehydrated and generally felt like my stomach was not handling things well.

I had bought a big stash of gels for the block:

- Precision Hydration 90g carb gels
- Precision 30g / 100mg caffeine gels
- SIS Beta Fuel Apple nootropics gels

I had used Precision before and had used SIS Beta Fuel before too, but not that specific Apple nootropics version. It tasted terrible, but I figured it was fine.

That ended up mattering a lot.

After the 50K, my shin hurt again badly enough that I was sure I had a stress fracture. I took three days off, felt slightly better, and went back to running anyway.

Two weeks before the race, I did a 20-miler with 6,000 feet of vert at night to practice for the dark sections. I felt awful afterward, slept badly, and then got hit again right before a three-day backpacking trip. Feverish, achy, unable to eat. Later we found out everyone had drunk bad water. It became an involuntary taper, and I spent the final stretch just hoping I would feel remotely normal by race day.

## Race week

I got to Chamonix on Wednesday morning after taking a train from Barcelona to Lyon on Tuesday and then an early bus from Lyon to Chamonix on Wednesday. On that bus I met Sebastian Chiu, another American running UTMB, and we ended up talking for hours.

Instead of diving straight into the circus, I met up with some Boston ultrarunner friends and headed to Courmayeur, where I was staying before the race. That day was mostly about doing as little as possible: relax, get a haircut, eat, and stay calm.

Thursday I went back to Chamonix to pick up my bib. That was my first real taste of UTMB week, and it is exactly as insane as people say. I met up with Coach Will, talked race strategy, walked the expo, got the last required gear I needed, and watched the OCC winners come through. The whole town felt electric. It was exciting, but I also understood immediately why people say the week itself can drain you before the race even starts.

On race day I got into Chamonix around 1 p.m. for the 6 p.m. start, dropped my bag, got lunch, and tried to sneak in a short nap.

Will had warned me to get to the start line early because the field is massive. I got there around 4:30 and was already deeper in the crowd than I expected. About 30 minutes before the gun, I took an SIS nootropics gel.

Then _Conquest of Paradise_ started playing, the whole start corral got goosebumps, and we were off.

## Race start

The first few miles of UTMB are unlike anything else.

Chamonix is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with people. Cowbells, screaming, total chaos. Even once you reach trail, there are runners everywhere. It is absurd in the best way.

And then almost immediately, I started feeling terrible.

I was moving fine at first. I took a full Precision 90g gel around 30 minutes in and hit Les Houches right around the split I expected. But my heart rate was stuck in the low-to-mid 150s and would not come down, even when I tried to back off. I could not get it into the 140s no matter what I did.

That is when my stomach started to go sideways too.

I felt awful in a way that made no sense that early in a race. Worse than I usually feel at mile 90. I wondered if I was just too amped from the crowds and the start, since I had been yelling back at people and feeding off the atmosphere. I tried to calm down, but nothing changed.

By Saint-Gervais, I was already hiding in the bathroom trying to regroup and texting Coach Will that things were not going well.

The next section is a blur. By the time I reached Les Contamines, I had thrown up everything, including lunch. I was well behind my goal split and the race already felt sideways. Somewhere in the runnable section from Saint-Gervais to Les Contamines, more than 300 people passed me.

Not exactly the plan.

## To Courmayeur

After Les Contamines, I finally found another American to talk to. His name was Patrick, and I pretty much unloaded my frustration onto him. He suggested I keep trying to get calories in, and after talking with him I managed to force down another gel.

We moved together for a while, and the conversation helped. That became a pattern for the race: when things were ugly, talking to someone often kept me moving.

One of the wildest parts of the course came next at Notre Dame de la Gorge, the so-called Fly Zone. It is complete madness: lights, music, people screaming, chainsaws, cowbells. It should have been one of the coolest moments of the race. I was still too busy feeling terrible to really enjoy it.

Later I ran into Sebastian again, which I had not expected at all. We chatted briefly, he got a quick video for the UTMB content he was filming, and I kept moving.

On the climb to Col du Bonhomme, I had very little power because I had barely eaten. But Patrick and I ended up talking for a long stretch, and the company helped. We passed a decent number of people on that climb mostly by just staying steady.

I felt better on the descent into Les Chapieux and took time there to refill carefully because the section to Lac Combal is long. I grabbed some apples and finally felt like maybe I could eat again.

Then I took another SIS Apple nootropics gel.

Almost immediately, everything came back up again.

That was the second major vomit episode, and it felt completely disproportionate to what I had just taken in. I was back to square one.

From there I mostly focused on getting from climb to climb. I found another runner, an Australian guy moving at a similar pace, and tucked in with him for a while. I was not climbing especially well, but I was surviving.

At Lac Combal I spent way too long sitting around, which also became a theme. UTMB aid stations are huge, covered, and full of chairs. That sounds nice, but it also makes it very easy to turn a quick stop into a 20-minute reset.

By the time the sun came up and I was descending toward Courmayeur, I knew I needed a real fix, not just another attempt at gutting it out.

## Courmayeur

I got into Courmayeur, found Will, sat down, and explained what had been happening.

He started going through the nutrition I had been carrying and asked which gels had been going down badly. Then he looked at the SIS nootropics packaging and basically said: _Wait, you have been taking these?_ The gel had around 200mg of caffeine plus a bunch of other ingredients I had not really paid attention to.

Suddenly a lot of the summer started making more sense.

The bad runs. The weird dehydration. The stomach problems. The vomiting. The terrible start to the race. I cannot prove it perfectly, but the correlation was strong enough that I am very comfortable blaming those gels.

So we threw the rest of them away immediately.

I stayed in Courmayeur for close to 45 minutes, which is a ridiculous amount of time, but I left in much better shape. My stomach had settled, I got in some soup and rice, and for the first time all race I felt like there was at least a chance I could work my way back into it.

## To the finish

Once I got moving again, things improved.

I climbed strongly up to Bertone, then moved across the ridge toward Bonatti and down to Arnouvaz. That whole section is beautiful, and once the stomach started cooperating, I could actually appreciate where I was. I was talking to people, sitting occasionally to force down gel like a shot, and generally trying to keep the race simple.

At Arnouvaz I regrouped again and then headed for the Gran Col Ferret, the hardest climb on the course, right in the heat of the day. I ended up climbing with Matt from Connecticut, which made the whole thing feel shorter.

After that climb, the long runnable descent toward La Fouly and Champex-Lac was finally a chance to race a little. I ran almost all the runnable sections and passed 55 people on that stretch alone.

I saw Will again at Champex-Lac and got ready for the final three climbs.

By that point a clear pattern had emerged:

- I was not climbing much faster than the people around me.
- I was descending much better than most of them.
- If the terrain got technical and rocky, I could keep running while other people started walking.

That is what kept me moving up through the second half.

The climb out of Champex-Lac felt rough and underpowered, but I made up ground again on the downhill to Trient. I reached Trient just after dark, and the aid station was a complete party. Someone named Caroline came in around the same time, and they started blasting “Sweet Caroline” through the station. Very good moment.

Then came the brutal climb out of Trient, around 2,500 feet in a little over two miles. I tucked in behind an Israeli runner who was talking nonstop and just let his pace drag me upward.

By Vallorcine it was around midnight and I thought I only had three or four hours left. Then Will told me it could still be more like 4.5 to 5 hours.

That did not exactly inspire joy.

But it did flip a switch. I decided I was not spending all night out there if I could help it.

I linked up for parts of the final climb with a French runner named Alexandre, moved well through the woods, and reached La Flégère with a shot at getting under 34 hours. From there it was about 4.5 miles back to Chamonix.

So I pushed.

My ankle had started tightening up after rolling it earlier, so I had to stay careful on the technical sections, but I still kept moving hard enough to hold the split I wanted.

Eventually I hit the bottom, passed the last runner I would overtake all night, climbed the metal stairs back across the road, and ran the final kilometer through Chamonix.

There were still people out cheering in the middle of the night.

And then it was done.

I finished UTMB in **33:36:57**.

Not the time goal I came for, but after how the race started, I was proud of it. I got it done and I finished strong.

Afterward I shivered through the finish area, collected the finisher vest, and then got the news that there was a problem with the Airbnb lockbox and we could not get inside. So naturally, we ended up sleeping on the ground outside the Airbnb door.

Completely ridiculous. Also completely fine. I was tired enough that I slept until 11 a.m. with no problem.

## Takeaways

A few very clear lessons came out of this one.

### 1. Never again with the nootropics gels

I am fully convinced those gels were a major factor in both the race issues and a lot of the stomach and hydration problems I had over the summer.

Never again.

### 2. UTMB is steeper than it looks on paper

On paper, my training looked specific enough. I was doing big vert, steep climbs, and mountain days. But UTMB is deceptive. The overall elevation profile is softened by long runnable sections, while the actual climbs are often brutally steep.

Next time, I would put even more focus on the steepest climbs I can find.

### 3. I spent way too long in aid stations

If I had to guess, I probably spent around 3.5 hours total in aid stations.

That is absurd.

European aid stations make it easy to sit, reset, and linger. Next time I race UTMB, I need to be in and out fast unless something is genuinely broken.

### 4. UTMB week is as good as advertised

The vibe is incredible. The whole town being taken over by trail and ultra running is exactly as fun as it sounds.

If I do it again, I would probably stay in Chamonix the whole time just to make logistics easier.

### 5. I want another shot at it

This race did not go the way I wanted, but it made me want to come back, not avoid it.

I will be back. Hopefully soon.
