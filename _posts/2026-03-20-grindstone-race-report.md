<div class="story-kicker">Grindstone 100 · Swoope, Virginia</div>

I did not plan to race another 100 that year.

After Western States, I had intentionally left the rest of the calendar open. I had no idea how I would feel after my first hundred, especially a hot one. People love to say it can take six months to feel normal again.

But three weeks later, I was running solid mileage again and feeling surprisingly good. The legs were sore on longer or harder efforts, sure, but nowhere near as trashed as I expected. Then Josh texted me that he had signed up for Grindstone and wanted me to crew and pace him.

My immediate reaction was: why not just run it?

That turned the whole thing into an experiment. I was in the middle of a bikepacking trip and it was going to be tough to train normally. I planned to keep running anyway, but the real question became this:

> Can you run a solid 100 miler off mostly bike training?

I booked a flight from San Diego back to DC for when I expected to be done with the GDMBR and decided to find out.

<hr class="story-divider" />

## Training

The recovery from Western States actually went well.

After two full weeks off, during which I dealt with some shin pain that started around mile 99 at States, I was already back to a 30-mile week. After three weeks, I was doing a speed workout. There was still some soreness when I started layering in harder and longer efforts, but that faded after a few weeks.

Before the bike tour started, I spent 10 days in Montana and then did a five-day training detour in Salmon, Idaho with a lot of slow, steep, vert-heavy climbing at altitude. I felt great on downhills and decent on flats, but climbing felt harder than it should have. Maybe it was altitude, lingering fatigue from Western States, or some combination of both.

Once the bike tour got going, the structure changed completely.

I ran about four times per week and averaged around 30 to 35 miles. Most mornings, I would wake up around 7 and go out for a six-mile jog on whatever roads or trails were near where I had camped. I tried to do hill strides when I could, but with all the residual fatigue from biking, I usually only managed one set of five or six strides each week, maybe two in a good week.

Almost every run felt like it was being done on tired legs. Constant heaviness, constant fatigue, lots of slow miles. If I could hit 9:30 pace on flats, I was happy.

The biking volume was no joke either. In my three biggest weeks, I averaged around 40 hours of riding, about 450 miles, and roughly 25,000 feet of gain on mostly dirt and gravel roads. It was a lot.

I still managed to get in a few key running efforts: some bigger mountain days in Idaho, an 11-miler on trails after the Tetons, a 24-mile partial night run in Breckenridge at 10,000 feet, a flat 18-miler in Albuquerque, and an 11-mile taper run with some vert in Los Angeles a week before the race.

The 24-miler gave me real confidence. I felt strong, practiced race-level fueling, and came out of it thinking maybe this weird build could actually work.

## Race start

The race started at 6 p.m. on Friday, which added a wrinkle I had never dealt with before.

I drove up with Josh, who was also racing, and Laura, who was crewing us. We picked up our bibs and ran into Max and Rosalinda Tiemann, who we had met earlier in the year at MMT100. Max is a very strong local runner who has won races like Georgia Death Race and No Business 100, and he has a reputation for running smart: conservative early, steady all day, strong late.

Josh and I hoped we could stay near Max for a while.

I camped with some new friends, Nancy and Jeff, who generously let me pitch a tent in their RV spot. Nancy was racing the 21K and had already done multiple Ironmans, so the whole campsite had a very endurance-nerd energy to it.

For food, I kept things simple: pasta the night before, two bagels with peanut butter and bananas plus coffee in the morning, Trader Joe’s rice snacks throughout the day, and a Panera chicken salad sandwich for lunch.

By the time the gun went off, I felt perfect.

Josh and I took off to Eminem’s “Till I Collapse,” which was a very American way to start a race that in my head still wanted to be UTMB. On the opening road miles we were moving around 8:30 pace, which honestly felt a little fast for me given how much of my recent training had been done closer to 9:30 pace on fatigued legs.

Yet even at that pace, there were still a ton of runners ahead of us.

That is one of the funniest things about the start of ultramarathons. You can have a pretty decent sense of where you stack up, and still there will be 30 extra people blasting off at a pace they absolutely are not going to hold. Once you have seen that enough times, it gets easier to relax and let the chaos happen.

Once we hit singletrack, I settled in immediately.

Josh and I ran together through the early conga lines, lost and found each other around an aid station, and eventually linked up with Max around mile 20. Temperatures were basically perfect, around 55 to 60 degrees, and for a while the three of us just chatted and enjoyed it.

I started feeling better on the Wild Oak Trail descent into North River Gap 1 at mile 32, the first crewed aid station. I had done that loop before, and having some familiarity with the trail helped.

## Feeling amazing

At North River Gap 1, Max, Josh, and I all took an extra minute. Rosalinda handled Max, Laura helped Josh and me, and it felt fun to be part of the same little race-within-the-race.

The next crew stop was only 13 miles away, so I took my first caffeine gel of the night, loaded a few more gels into my pack, and headed back out with Josh.

A little later I told him, “your turn to lead,” because I had been pulling for a while. He kept things steady, but before long I started feeling incredible and surged ahead after he helped me replace my headlamp batteries.

Sadly, I would not see Josh again during the race.

For a stretch, I felt as good as I ever have in an ultra.

I was flying on that kind of flattish, winding singletrack that I love most, clicking off sub-10 GAP miles and barely having to think about it. I chatted with another runner, took the more runnable downhill into Dowells Draft at mile 46 under control, and rolled into the aid feeling confident.

It had started lightly raining by then, but nothing too dramatic.

Rosalinda and Laura got me resupplied. Max had only left a few minutes earlier, and I told Laura I thought Josh might be 20 minutes behind me based on how that section had gone. Then I loaded up for the long 26-mile lollipop loop and headed back out.

The loop started gently enough before turning into a long sustained climb. I caught a runner named Chris, talked with him for a while, and then moved ahead once the hiking started. He gave me some useful course intel before I passed him, which I appreciated. Later on that climb I also passed the first female.

On the way down into the aid station, I spotted Max just ahead, getting ready to blow straight through. I called his name, he eased up a little, and I ducked in quickly to top off fluids. Catching him again felt like proof that I was moving really well.

<hr class="story-divider" />

## Temperature management and nutrition

Max and I spent a good part of the next climb together, mostly united in our shared opinion that the lollipop loop was kind of evil.

It was hard in every annoying way: rainy, cold and windy on the ridges, warmer in the valleys, foggy enough that visibility was limited, narrow sidehill trail in places, and classic East Coast wet rocks everywhere. It was slow, slippery, and mentally draining.

One thing I do think I handled well all night was temperature management.

I kept making a point to take my rain jacket and gloves on and off as conditions changed. That sounds minor, but it mattered because it helped keep my stomach working. At one point on the loop my stomach started to feel a little uneasy and I skipped a gel. Then I noticed my hands were cold, put on the jacket and gloves, and after about 30 minutes I was able to eat again without any issues.

The same basic thing happened one other time later in the race. Once I got warm enough again, I could keep fueling.

Overall, the nutrition was strong.

I used a mix of SIS Beta Fuel gels and Precision Hydration gels, took a few 250 mg salt caps per hour early on, and backed off those once the weather got colder. I had three caffeine gels total: 100 mg around mile 33, another 100 mg sometime during the lollipop loop, and then 150 mg right before the climb out of the loop.

That last caffeine hit did not sit well.

I started feeling dizzy and like my breathing and heart rate were off, so I shut caffeine down after that. Still, on the whole, the fueling was good. My rough math afterward said I averaged about 270 calories per hour from gels alone, and probably more like 300 to 350 per hour if I count what I actually managed to get from the on-course Naak.

That is right in the zone I want.

The bigger problem was climbing.

Somewhere on the way out of the loop, Max finally dropped me. I was breathing hard, struggling to settle down, and suddenly my best skill in these races, climbing, was not really working.

That was the start of the fade.

<hr class="story-divider" />

## Just keep moving

I came back into Dowells Draft 2 and found that Max had already left by a few minutes.

I changed shoes, took a slightly longer stop while Laura and Rosalinda repacked my gels and bottles, and made the decision to carry everything I would need for the next few sections in case Laura had to stay back longer with Josh and missed the next crewed stop.

It was probably my longest aid station of the race, maybe five minutes.

Leaving the aid, Chris, the runner I had been with earlier, passed me with his pacer and I tucked in behind them. We quickly moved past another runner and picked up a place. Chris was running a lot of the uphills while I was mostly hiking, but somehow I could still keep him within sight, and once the trail tipped downhill again, I came back by him.

The 5.5 miles of gravel road to Camp Todd were annoying, but I still ran about 95 percent of it.

Then came the big climb up to Little Bald on the Wild Oak Trail, around 3,000 feet of climbing, and that was where things got noticeably harder. I was struggling to hold even 22-minute pace uphill and started needing short 20-second breathers on the side of the trail just to get my breathing back under control.

Paul Jacobs passed me there and we exchanged a few encouraging words. A little later I ran a few miles with Zach, a 100K runner from Montana who was also having a rough day, and once again I noticed how much easier it felt to keep moving when I had company.

That might have been the moment I started thinking more seriously that maybe I should have had a pacer.

Going into the mile 93 aid station, I asked what place I was in. They told me I was eighth, and that seventh place was still in the aid station. I got in and out quickly enough to leave ahead of him and his pacer.

<hr class="story-divider" />

## Is it over yet?

The final stretch was not exactly glamorous.

More 100K runners started coming by, including the top two women, and it was genuinely cool to watch their race unfolding even while I was deep in my own mess. I had to walk too much of the final ugly uphill, and in hindsight that is one of the clearest spots where I know I could have gone deeper.

Eventually I hit the last road section and knew there were about 3.5 miles left.

At that point, I really wanted to coast. Walk it in. Be done.

Instead, I stopped to pee, let a 100K runner go by, and then looked back.

The guy I had just jumped for seventh place was coming with his pacer.

That changed the mood instantly.

I basically went: okay, enough of that.

I started running around 8:30 pace and committed to it. After a couple miles, I had shaken him. Then I checked the watch and realized I still had a shot at sub-23 hours if I kept pressing.

So I kept pressing.

I made it to the finish in **22:58:29**, good for **7th place**.

Per usual, my legs shut down almost immediately once I stopped moving.

Some local runners who were volunteering and spectating congratulated me, my RV friends helped me limp back to camp in the pouring rain, and I took a hot shower while freezing and shivering. Josh later told me he had dropped at mile 85 after a bad ankle sprain, which was a brutal outcome after how strong his buildup had looked.

Jeff made me steak and eggs, Nancy told me about her own race, where she had finished second in her age group, and I did my best to stay awake for the conversation.

I had been up for roughly 36 hours, maybe the longest continuous stretch of my life.

The next morning I slept hard, got picked up by Josh and Laura, crushed a huge Cracker Barrel meal, and met back up with Max and Rosalinda to talk through the race.

Max had run an excellent final third and finished fifth, about an hour ahead of me.

He absolutely crushed the late miles.

## Takeaways

My goal for this race was not really about time.

It was about pushing deeper.

I had been really happy with my result at Western States, but I also knew I had paced that race conservatively because I was still learning the 100-mile distance. At Grindstone, I wanted to find the edge a little more, see when the wheels might start to come off, and see whether I could keep pressing once they did.

On that front, I think the race delivered.

I went hard for about 30 miles, paid for it later, and still managed to move from 10th to 7th when I felt like I was absolutely crawling on the late climbs. It reinforced something Jim Walmsley once said after UTMB: when he was hurting, Germain was hurting too, and that gave him confidence.

That feels true in every hundred.

By mile 75, everyone is suffering. If you can just keep moving, you still have a real shot to hold position or move up.

Of course, there is still a lot to improve.

### 1. I can go deeper late in the race

I wish I had run more of the final climb and the last gravel road section. If I had, maybe I could have gotten closer to the runner ahead of me.

The good news is that the pain cave is bigger now than it was before.

### 2. My stoppage time was way too high

According to Strava, I had 21:54 of moving time.

That means I gave away a little over an hour in non-moving time.

I was happy with my aid station management overall, and I do not think I spent more than 20 to 30 minutes total in aid stations. So the rest came from everything else: bathroom stops, headlamp fixes, jacket changes, quick side-of-trail breaks, and generally doing too much stationary problem-solving.

That has to improve.

When you compare that gap against stronger runners, it is obvious how much free time I was donating. That is the kind of thing that quietly separates a solid result from a really competitive one.

### 3. I need to get faster on flats

After spending time around Max during the race, one thing stood out clearly: his baseline cruising speed on flatter terrain is just faster than mine.

I feel very confident in my power hiking and descending. But if I want to level up, I need better general speed.

Given that I was going to be spending the next stretch of life bikepacking through Central and South America without much trail access, that actually felt like a useful direction: get faster on roads, sharpen that tool, and bring it back to the trails later.

### 4. The bike-training experiment worked

This was the big question coming in, and I think the answer was yes.

You really can prepare for an ultra mostly off bike training, at least if the bike volume is high enough and you keep enough running in the mix to stay specific.

All of that long Z1 and Z2 work on the bike clearly transferred. It let me stay durable, keep moving, and still race late even when the climbing started to fall apart.

That gives me a lot of confidence.

I do not think I am that far away from competing for the podium in strong 100-mile fields. I just need to clean up the details, get faster on the flats, and waste less time.
